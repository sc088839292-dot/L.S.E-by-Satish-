Module I — Self-Awareness and Personal Growth

1. Introduction to Life Skills

Life skills are abilities that help a person deal effectively and responsibly with everyday demands, relationships, studies and work. They include thinking, social and emotional skills.

  • Thinking: critical and creative thinking, decision-making, problem-solving.
  • Social: communication, empathy, cooperation, relationship skills.
  • Emotional: self-awareness, emotional regulation, resilience and stress management.

Importance: life skills improve confidence, adaptability, healthy relationships, academic performance, employability and responsible choices. They develop through practice, reflection and feedback.

2. Character Building

Character is the set of values, habits and qualities guiding behaviour. Character building is the gradual development of positive qualities through repeated actions.

Honesty: speaking truthfully and avoiding cheating.
Integrity: doing the right thing even without supervision.
Responsibility: accepting duties and consequences.
Respect: valuing others' dignity, time and opinions.
Discipline: following useful routines with self-control.
Empathy: understanding another person's viewpoint.

Ways: set standards, keep promises, be punctual, admit mistakes, learn from role models, help others and reflect on daily actions.

3. Understanding Self: Self-Awareness

Self-awareness means understanding one's emotions, thoughts, values, strengths, weaknesses, interests and habits, and recognising how behaviour affects others. It helps set realistic goals and improve relationships.

Develop it through: journaling, feedback from trusted people, identifying emotional triggers, observing habits, skills assessments and reviewing successes/mistakes.

Example: A student notices that delaying assignments causes stress. Recognising the habit is self-awareness; making a timetable is an action based on it.

4. Personality Types and Traits

Personality is the relatively consistent pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving that makes a person distinctive. It is influenced by biology, upbringing, culture, experience and environment.

Broad types: introversion (often prefers quieter settings and reflection), extraversion (often enjoys social interaction), ambiversion (a mix depending on situation). These are tendencies, not fixed labels; neither is better.

5. Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to recognise, understand and manage one's own emotions and respond thoughtfully to others' emotions.

  1. Self-awareness: identify what you feel and why.
  2. Self-regulation: pause and choose a suitable response.
  3. Motivation: work towards goals despite setbacks.
  4. Empathy: understand another person's feelings and viewpoint.
  5. Social skills: communicate, cooperate and manage relationships.

Benefits: better teamwork, conflict handling, leadership, stress management and decisions. Improve EI by naming emotions, listening, considering viewpoints and reflecting after difficult situations.

6. Self-Regulation

Self-regulation is managing emotions, attention, impulses and behaviour to meet goals and follow values. It does not mean suppressing every emotion; it means responding in a balanced way.

Steps: notice the trigger; pause and breathe; name the emotion; consider outcomes; select a respectful response; review what worked. For study, break work into small tasks, reduce distractions and track progress.

7. Personal Values and Ethics

Values are principles considered important, such as honesty, fairness, respect, compassion and responsibility. Ethics are standards used to judge right and wrong conduct.

Importance: values guide priorities and decisions; ethics protect trust and fairness in education, relationships and workplaces.

Ethical decision process: identify the issue; collect facts; identify people affected; consider rules and values; compare options and consequences; choose a fair action; review the result.

Example: If a friend asks for test answers, honesty and fairness suggest refusing to help them cheat while offering to study together later.

8. Goal Setting

A goal is a desired result that directs effort. SMART goals are:

Break long-term goals into milestones, identify resources, anticipate obstacles and review progress.

9. Time Management

Time management is planning and controlling how time is used. It reduces last-minute pressure and balances study, rest and responsibilities.

  • List tasks and estimate duration.
  • Prioritise by importance and urgency.
  • Use weekly timetable and daily task list.
  • Break large tasks into focused sessions.
  • Keep buffer time and limit distractions.
  • Review the plan at day's end.

Eisenhower Matrix: urgent+important: do now; important+not urgent: schedule; urgent+less important: delegate/limit; neither: reduce/remove. Pomodoro: focus for a planned interval (often 25 minutes), take a short break, and after several cycles take a longer break.

Module II — Effective Communication

1. Basics of Interpersonal Communication

Communication is exchanging information, ideas and feelings to create shared understanding. Interpersonal communication occurs between two or more people.

Cycle: sender develops idea → encodes message → sends through channel → receiver decodes → feedback. Noise or barriers can disturb any stage. Elements include sender, message, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback and context.

Good communication should be clear, complete, correct, concise, courteous and considerate.

2. Barriers to Communication

  • Physical: noise, distance, poor network.
  • Language/semantic: jargon, unfamiliar words, ambiguity.
  • Psychological: stress, fear, anger, assumptions.
  • Cultural: differences in norms, gestures and expectations.
  • Organisational: unclear roles, hierarchy, information overload.

Solutions: simple language, active listening, suitable channel, checking understanding, inviting questions, respecting cultural differences and timely feedback.

3. Active Listening Skills

Active listening means giving full attention, understanding the message and responding thoughtfully rather than merely hearing words.

  • Face the speaker and minimise distractions.
  • Do not interrupt; let the speaker finish.
  • Use attentive body language and suitable eye contact.
  • Ask open questions for clarity.
  • Paraphrase the main idea in your own words.
  • Reflect feelings respectfully and summarise key points.

Example: “So the slides are needed one day earlier. Have I understood correctly?” Benefits include trust, fewer misunderstandings and better conflict resolution.

4. Verbal Communication

Verbal communication uses spoken or written words. Oral examples: conversation, meetings, interviews and presentations. Written examples: emails, reports and messages.

Effective practice: choose familiar words, organise ideas logically, speak at suitable pace and volume, use respectful tone, give relevant examples and check understanding. Avoid vague statements and unnecessary jargon.

5. Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal communication conveys meaning without words and may support or change spoken meaning.

6. Public Speaking

Public speaking is presenting information or ideas to an audience, such as in classroom talks, seminars and meetings.

Preparation: identify purpose and audience; research; organise introduction, main points and conclusion; prepare examples; practise aloud; check timing and equipment.

Delivery: begin clearly; speak steadily; look around the audience naturally; use purposeful gestures; avoid reading every word; handle questions politely.

Managing nervousness: prepare, rehearse, breathe slowly, arrive early, use cue cards and focus on helping the audience. Confidence grows with practice.

7. Presentation Skills

A presentation communicates information through speech and supporting visuals such as slides, charts or demonstrations.

  • One main idea per slide; readable fonts and contrast.
  • Use short bullets, diagrams and relevant visuals, not paragraphs.
  • Keep layout consistent; avoid distracting animation.
  • Explain visuals instead of reading them aloud.
  • Rehearse transitions, timing and equipment.
  • End with summary and questions.

Structure: title/purpose → context → key points with evidence → summary → questions/closing.

8. Written Communication in Professional Settings

Includes emails, notices, letters, reports, memos and meeting minutes. It should be accurate, organised, polite and suitable for the reader.

Professional email: meaningful subject; appropriate greeting; brief opening stating purpose; clear body with details/action; polite closing; name/signature. Check spelling, attachments, recipients and tone.

Principles: complete sentences, simple language, correct facts, logical sequence and useful headings. Avoid slang, unnecessary abbreviations and emotional wording.

9. Conflict Resolution

Conflict is disagreement due to different needs, goals, values, expectations or limited resources. Respectful handling can reveal problems and improve solutions.

Steps: stay calm; define the issue without blame; let each side explain; listen and identify shared interests; generate options; agree on fair action; review results.

Approaches: collaboration (win-win), compromise (each gives something), accommodation (one yields when appropriate), avoidance (pause/defer if timing is unsuitable), competition/assertion (firm decision when necessary). Choose based on urgency, importance and relationship.

10. Negotiation

Negotiation is discussion between parties to reach agreement when interests differ. Effective negotiation seeks a fair, practical outcome.

  1. Prepare needs, priorities, facts and acceptable limits.
  2. Understand the other side's interests.
  3. Discuss respectfully and ask questions.
  4. Suggest options and compare benefits/costs.
  5. Seek mutual gains and objective criteria.
  6. Confirm agreement, responsibilities and timeline.

Example: Project teammates compare availability and strengths, agree on fair task division and set deadlines.

Module III — Critical Thinking and Problem Solving

1. Introduction to Critical Thinking

Critical thinking is careful, logical and fair evaluation of information, arguments and evidence before forming a judgement. It is not simply criticising; it means asking questions and checking reasons.

Characteristics: curiosity, open-mindedness, clarity, accuracy, relevance, logical reasoning, evidence-based judgement and willingness to revise an opinion.

Process: identify question; collect information; check source reliability; separate facts from opinions; examine assumptions/evidence; compare explanations; draw a reasoned conclusion.

2. Analytical Reasoning

Analytical reasoning breaks complex information into parts and examines relationships, patterns, causes and effects.

Methods: classify information; compare similarities/differences; identify trends; break a problem into components; use tables/diagrams/data; test whether evidence supports a conclusion.

Example: If attendance and marks both change, do not assume one caused the other. Check preparation, teaching methods and sample size too.

3. Logical Reasoning

Logical reasoning uses valid steps to reach conclusions from facts or premises.

Deductive: general rule to specific conclusion. Example: all assignments need a cover page; this is an assignment; therefore it needs one (if the rule applies).
Inductive: observations to a probable generalisation. Several buses were late; the route may often be delayed, but more evidence is needed.
Abductive: selecting the most plausible explanation for observations while recognising it may be wrong.

Common fallacies: personal attack instead of addressing an argument; hasty generalisation from too few examples; false dilemma presenting only two choices; popularity treated as proof; confusing correlation with causation.

4. Creative Problem-Solving Techniques

Creative problem-solving develops new and useful ways to address a challenge by combining imagination with testing.

  1. Define: state problem and goal clearly.
  2. Explore: collect facts and understand users/constraints.
  3. Generate: brainstorm ideas without judging too early.
  4. Select: compare usefulness, cost, time and feasibility.
  5. Prototype: make a small model or trial version.
  6. Test/improve: gather feedback, revise and implement.

Techniques: brainstorming; mind mapping; SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange); 5 Whys for root causes; design thinking (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test).

5. Decision-Making Strategies

Decision-making is selecting the best available option from alternatives to achieve a goal.

Steps: define decision; identify criteria; collect reliable information; list alternatives; compare advantages, disadvantages, risks and consequences; choose; act; review outcome.

StrategyUse
Pros and consCompare benefits and drawbacks.
Decision matrixScore options against weighted criteria such as cost, time and quality.
Cost-benefit analysisCompare expected benefits with money, time and effort.

ConsultationSeek relevant expertise or stakeholder views.

Types: programmed/routine decisions follow established rules; non-programmed decisions require judgement and new analysis. Avoid impulsiveness, confirmation bias and ignoring long-term effects.

6. Information Literacy

Information literacy is the ability to recognise an information need and locate, evaluate, use and communicate information responsibly.

Process: define need; choose keywords and sources; search; evaluate credibility, accuracy, authority, purpose and date; compare sources; record references; use information ethically and avoid plagiarism.

Check sources: Who created it? What expertise/evidence is shown? When was it published? Is the purpose to inform, sell or persuade? Can claims be verified elsewhere?

7. Media Awareness

Media awareness/media literacy means understanding how media messages are created, distributed and interpreted, and evaluating their accuracy, purpose and influence.

  • Distinguish fact, opinion, advertisement, satire and sponsored content.
  • Check original source, author, date and evidence.
  • Read beyond headlines and compare independent sources.
  • Watch for edited images, misleading charts, emotional language and missing context.
  • Verify surprising claims before sharing; do not forward unverified messages.
  • Respect privacy, copyright and people's dignity online.

Misinformation is inaccurate information shared regardless of intent; disinformation is deliberately created/shared to mislead. Verification matters because both can cause harm.

8. Practical Problem-Solving Example

Problem: a student project is repeatedly late. Define: tasks and deadlines are unclear. Analyse: list tasks, owners, dependencies and time estimates. Generate: shared task board, smaller milestones and weekly check-ins. Decide: choose a shared tracker. Implement: assign owners and dates. Review: compare progress with plan and adjust.
